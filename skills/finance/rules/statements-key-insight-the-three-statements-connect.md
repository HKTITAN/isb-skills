impact: HIGH

- Net Income from Income Statement increases equity on Balance Sheet
- Balance Sheet items (receivables, inventory changes) explain why Net Income ≠ Cash on Cash Flow Statement
- Cash changes appear on Balance Sheet

**Example: Coffee Shop Year 1**

Income Statement:
```
Revenue: $100,000
COGS: $40,000
Gross Profit: $60,000
Operating Expenses: $45,000
Operating Income: $15,000
Interest Expense: $2,000
Taxes: $2,600
Net Income: $10,400
```

Cash Flow Statement:
```
Operating Activities:
  Net Income: $10,400
  Add: Depreciation: $2,000
  Subtract: AR increase: $(5,000)
  Operating Cash Flow: $7,400

Investing Activities:
  CapEx: $(3,000)
  Investing Cash Flow: $(3,000)

Financing Activities:
  Debt issuance: $2,000
  Financing Cash Flow: $2,000

Net change in cash: $6,400
```

Balance Sheet:
```
Assets:
  Cash: $6,400
  AR: $5,000
  Inventory: $8,000
  Equipment: $12,000
  Total: $31,400

Liabilities:
  AP: $10,000
  Short-term debt: $2,000
  Long-term debt: $3,000
  Total: $15,000

Equity:
  Beginning: $10,000
  + Net Income: $10,400
  = Ending: $20,400

Total Liabilities + Equity: $35,400
```

**Important: Quality of Earnings**

Never trust net income alone. High-quality earnings:
- Match revenue recognition timing with cash collection (receivables growing slower than revenue)
- Use conservative accounting estimates (realistic depreciation, reasonable bad debt allowances)
- Minimize one-time items and accounting adjustments
- Generate positive operating cash flow (OCF > Net Income is good; OCF < Net Income signals earnings manipulation)
