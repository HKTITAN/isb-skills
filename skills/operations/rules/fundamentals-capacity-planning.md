**Capacity Types**:
- Design (Theoretical) capacity: Maximum possible output under ideal conditions (no downtime)
- Effective capacity: Realistic utilization accounting for maintenance, setup, breaks, changeovers
- Actual (Demonstrated) capacity: Historical average output achieved in practice
- Utilization rate: Actual output ÷ Design capacity (often 60-80% is realistic; >80% creates bottlenecks and variability)

**Capacity Efficiency Metrics**:
- Overall Equipment Effectiveness (OEE) = Availability × Performance × Quality
  - Availability: % uptime (downtime / total time)
  - Performance: Actual output ÷ Design output
  - Quality: Good units ÷ Total units (excludes rework)
- Benchmark: World-class manufacturers target OEE > 85%

**Capacity Strategy** (multi-year decision):
- Lead strategy: Build ahead of demand (never lose sales due to capacity, but excess capacity costs high)
- Match strategy: Build to meet current demand (efficient asset utilization, balanced capital investment)
- Lag strategy: Build after demand emerges (minimal excess capacity, but initial constraints and lost sales)
- Choice depends on: Capital intensity, demand growth rate, price elasticity, competitive intensity

**Peak vs. Average Demand Management**: Most operations have uneven demand.
- Option 1: Size for average demand; accept long wait times/delays at peaks (loose service level)
- Option 2: Size for peak demand; accept idle capacity during troughs (loose asset utilization)
- Option 3: Use temporary capacity (outsourcing, temporary workers, overtime) to handle peaks
- Optimal typically balances: Service level + asset utilization + flexibility costs

**Capacity Flexibility**:
- Permanent capacity: Difficult/expensive to adjust (fixed investment)
- Flexible capacity: Easier to adjust (temporary workers, subcontracting, shifting to different product)
- Importance: Dynamic environments benefit from flexible capacity even if slightly more expensive
