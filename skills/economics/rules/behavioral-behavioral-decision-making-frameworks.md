**System 1 vs System 2 Thinking (Kahneman)**
- System 1: Fast, intuitive, automatic, emotional, requires little effort, error-prone for complex decisions
- System 2: Slow, deliberate, logical, effortful, high cognitive load, accurate but energy-intensive
- Decision-making: System 1 used most of time; System 2 engaged for complex decisions
- Bias sources: System 1 heuristics often unreliable; System 2 can correct but requires effort
- Design implication: Leverage System 1 for simple, frequent decisions; enable System 2 for complex choices
- Effort aversion: People avoid cognitive effort; rely on heuristics and defaults when possible

**Bounded Rationality**
- Definition: Decision-makers have limited information, cognitive capacity, and time
- Satisficing: Choosing first acceptable option rather than optimizing over all alternatives
- Information costs: Rarely rational to gather complete information; optimal information varies
- Complexity: Real-world decisions involve uncertainty, multiple objectives, strategic interactions
- Implication: Perfect rationality assumption unrealistic; must account for cognitive limitations
- Search costs: Time and money to find information creates stopping rules before perfect search

**Heuristics and When They Fail**
- Useful but fallible: Heuristics save cognitive effort; useful when applied appropriately
- Failures: Biases occur when heuristics applied in situations where they mislead
- Effort-accuracy tradeoff: More accurate decisions require more cognitive effort
- Context matters: Expertise and domain knowledge improve heuristic reliability
- Training: Structured decision processes and checklists can mitigate bias in high-stakes decisions
