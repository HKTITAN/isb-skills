**Central Bank Tools**
- Open Market Operations (OMOs): Buying and selling government bonds to change money supply
  - Expansionary: Buy bonds → Inject money → Lower rates → Encourage borrowing/spending
  - Contractionary: Sell bonds → Remove money → Raise rates → Discourage borrowing
- Reserve Requirements: How much cash banks must hold vs. lend out
  - Lower requirements → Banks lend more → More money supply → Lower rates
  - Higher requirements → Banks lend less → Less money supply → Higher rates
- Discount Rate: Interest rate charged when banks borrow from central bank
  - Lower rate → Banks borrow cheaply → Lend more → More spending
  - Higher rate → Banks borrow expensively → Lend less → Less spending

**Interest Rates and Economic Activity**
- Low rates encourage: Borrowing, investment, stock market rises, consumption
- High rates discourage: Borrowing, investment, stock market often falls, consumption
- Transmission mechanism: Rate changes → borrowing cost changes → investment/consumption changes → demand changes → inflation/output changes
- Lags: Policy effects take 6-18 months to fully manifest; central banks must forecast and act in advance

**Quantitative Easing**
- Purpose: When policy rates hit zero bound, central banks buy long-term bonds (treasuries, corporate bonds)
- Mechanism: Increases money supply, lowers long-term rates, encourages investment and spending
- Asset price inflation: Stocks, real estate rise because investors have lots of cheap money
- Wealth effect: Rich people feel richer; increase spending
- Inflation risk: Too much money chasing same goods causes inflation if continued too long

**Inflation Targeting**
- Framework: Central bank commits to target inflation rate (typically 2-3%); adjusts policy accordingly
- Credibility: Inflation expectations become anchored; easier to control actual inflation
- Flexibility: Central banks target inflation but also consider unemployment/output
- Forward guidance: Communicates future policy path; manages expectations

**Monetary Policy Trilemma**
- The constraint: Cannot simultaneously have fixed exchange rate, free capital flows, and independent monetary policy
- Combinations: Choose any two of three; the third is sacrificed
- Fixed exchange rate + free flows → must match foreign monetary policy; no independence
- Fixed exchange rate + independence → must restrict capital flows
- Free flows + independence → must allow flexible exchange rate
