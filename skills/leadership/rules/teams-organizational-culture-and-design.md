**Culture elements**: The actual operating norms (not the stated values) that drive behavior:

- How decisions really get made (who has input, how consensus forms)
- What behaviors are rewarded/punished (regardless of stated values)
- How mistakes are handled (learning or blame?)
- How diversity/dissent is treated (embraced or suppressed?)
- How success is defined and celebrated
- How failures are processed
- What informal networks and relationships matter
- How people spend their time and attention
- What stories get told about the organization

**Culture is self-reinforcing**: Culture perpetuates itself through:
1. **Hiring and Promotion**: Who do we hire? People tend to hire those similar to themselves. Promotion criteria reinforce cultural norms. A culture of long hours reinforces when workaholics get promoted.

2. **Systems and Processes**: Metrics, approval processes, decision-making structures encode culture. Slow approval processes reinforce caution. Decentralized budgets reinforce initiative.

3. **Stories and Symbols**: Which leaders are celebrated? What stories do we tell about heroes and failures? Celebration of rule-breakers who succeeded signals entrepreneurship; celebration of process-followers signals stability.

4. **Informal Networks**: Who talks to whom? Whose advice is sought? Power distribution reflected in networks.

5. **Leader Behavior**: Leaders are watched constantly. Stating "we value work-life balance" while working 70-hour weeks signals ambition is what matters. Leaders quickly reveal true culture.

**Culture is resistant to change** because all elements reinforce each other. Stating new values without changing hiring, promotion, metrics, and leader behavior changes nothing. Real culture change requires simultaneous changes across multiple elements.

**Changing culture requires**:
1. Clear new cultural aspiration (what will be different?)
2. Leadership alignment and visible modeling of new behaviors
3. Hiring and promotion criteria aligned to new culture
4. New metrics that measure what you want to encourage
5. Removing/redirecting cultural carriers of old culture (who embodies old norms?)
6. New stories and symbols that reinforce new culture
7. Patient persistence (culture change takes 2-3 years minimum)

**When culture change fails**: Usually because leaders change only one element (new values statement) without changing hiring, promotion, metrics, and their own behavior.

**Schein's Three Levels of Culture**:

1. **Artifacts** (visible): Policies, physical space, language, dress, stories, rituals
2. **Values** (espoused): What the organization says it believes
3. **Assumptions** (basic): Underlying beliefs about reality, human nature, relationships - these drive actual behavior

Most culture change work fails because leaders change artifacts (new policy, new office layout) without shifting basic assumptions. Real culture change requires changing who gets hired, who gets promoted, whose stories get told, what gets celebrated.

**Organizational design models**:

1. **Hierarchical/Functional**: Functions (engineering, sales, finance) as silos; efficient for stable environments, slow innovation
   - Strengths: Clear accountability, deep expertise development, efficient resource utilization
   - Weaknesses: Slow cross-functional decisions, siloed thinking, limited customer perspective
   - When to use: Stable business, standardized processes, efficiency priority

2. **Divisional**: Business units by product/market; enables local decision-making but duplicates functions
   - Strengths: Local autonomy, faster decisions, accountability per unit
   - Weaknesses: Duplicate functions increase cost, inconsistent standards, less expertise depth
   - When to use: Multiple distinct markets, different customer needs per division

3. **Matrix**: Report to both function and business unit; enables both efficiency and responsiveness but creates role confusion and conflict
   - Strengths: Combines functional expertise with business focus
   - Weaknesses: Ambiguous authority, political conflicts, slow decisions when matrices unclear
   - When to use: Need both efficiency and responsiveness; only if clear decision rights defined

4. **Network/Agile**: Flat, cross-functional teams; fast innovation and adaptation but requires strong culture and clear decision rights
   - Strengths: Fast decisions, innovation, flexibility
   - Weaknesses: Requires mature culture, can lack accountability, difficult to scale
   - When to use: High uncertainty, need innovation, strong culture already exists

**Design trade-offs**: All designs involve fundamental tradeoffs:
- **Efficiency vs. Innovation**: Hierarchy efficient; networks innovative
- **Accountability vs. Flexibility**: Clear hierarchy has clear accountability; networks flexible but accountability diffuse
- **Depth vs. Breadth**: Functional expertise deep; divisional model enables breadth
- **Consistency vs. Local Responsiveness**: Hierarchy provides consistency; divisional enables local adaptation

**Reorganization patterns**: Organizations oscillate between:
- Centralization (for efficiency, consistency, control)
- Decentralization (for speed, empowerment, local responsiveness)

Each creates conditions for the other. When centralized, business units push for autonomy and speed. When decentralized, companies lose efficiency and need centralization. Smart leaders manage oscillation based on strategic priorities and organizational capability.

**Competing Values Framework** (Quinn): Organizations emphasize different value combinations:
- **Clan Culture** (family-like): Emphasize collaboration, teamwork, participation, mentoring
- **Adhocracy Culture** (entrepreneurial): Emphasize innovation, risk-taking, creativity, rapid change
- **Market Culture** (results-driven): Emphasize competition, achievement, results, efficiency
- **Hierarchy Culture** (process-oriented): Emphasize stability, rules, procedures, control

Most effective organizations balance all four. Imbalance (all hierarchy, no adhocracy) limits growth; (all adhocracy, no hierarchy) creates chaos.

**Control vs. coordination tension**: Hierarchies control through rules and authority; networks coordinate through shared understanding. High-uncertainty environments need networks; high-consistency environments can use hierarchy. Most organizations need both.

**Reorganization patterns**: Organizations often oscillate between centralization (for efficiency/control) and decentralization (for speed/empowerment). Each creates the conditions for the other. Effective leaders choose the right time for changes based on organizational capability and strategic need.
