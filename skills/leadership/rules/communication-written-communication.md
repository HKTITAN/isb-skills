**Business writing principle**: Conclusions first, not at the end.

**The Pyramid Principle**:
- Lead with recommendation/conclusion
- Support with key arguments
- Back up with evidence

**Structure of a persuasive argument**:
1. **Situation**: Establish shared understanding of current state
2. **Complication**: Identify what's wrong or what opportunity exists
3. **Resolution**: Propose your answer
4. **Benefit**: What will improve when implemented

This structure is more persuasive than beginning with data and building to conclusion.

**Persuasive writing elements**:

**Credibility (Ethos)**: Audiences must believe you're trustworthy and knowledgeable
- Cite quality sources
- Show you understand opposing views
- Use precise language; avoid hyperbole
- Acknowledge limitations
- Demonstrate expertise through appropriate depth

**Logic and Evidence (Logos)**: Arguments must hold together rationally
- Each claim supported by data or sound reasoning
- Logical progression where each point builds on prior ones
- Acknowledge alternative interpretations; explain why yours is stronger
- Quantify claims when possible
- Use analogies to help audience understand complex ideas

**Emotional Connection (Pathos)**: Business audiences respond to values alignment and stories
- Frame challenges in terms audience cares about
- Use concrete examples and stories
- Connect to organizational values
- Acknowledge human side of implementation
- Show why this matters beyond metrics

**Visual communication in written work**:
- Use headers and subheaders to guide reader
- Bold key conclusions and recommendations
- White space improves readability
- Consistent formatting builds credibility
- Short paragraphs beat long ones
- Vary sentence length for readability

**Business writing genres**:

**Email**: 
- Subject line is everything (specific, benefit-focused, action-oriented)
- Critical content in first three lines
- One decision per email
- Respect reader attention; use bold, bullets, white space
- Clear next steps
- Sign-off establishes tone

**Business proposal**:
- Executive summary (decision requested, expected benefit, investment, timeline)
- Situation analysis (current state, why it matters)
- Solution description (clear, specific, implementable)
- Business case (impact, ROI, strategic fit)
- Implementation plan (timeline, resources, risks, mitigation)
- Risk analysis (what could go wrong, how you'll prevent/address)
- Appendices (detailed data, supporting analysis)

**Case analysis** (framework for analyzing business scenarios):
- Situation summary (100-150 words establishing context)
- Problem identification (core issue, root causes, not symptoms)
- Strategic analysis (industry dynamics, competitive position, key success factors)
- Alternative approaches (2-3 options with pros/cons of each)
- Recommendation (specific, implementable, addresses root cause)
- Implementation (first steps, timeline, obstacles, resource requirements)

**Case method discussion**: Case method develops judgment through structured analysis of real business situations. When discussing cases:
- Listen before speaking; understand others' perspectives
- Challenge assumptions (your own and others') constructively
- Use data to support positions but acknowledge uncertainty
- Consider multiple perspectives before concluding
- Test recommendations against realities of execution

**Feedback delivery using SBI**:
Situation-Behavior-Impact model makes feedback more effective than vague comments:
- Situation: "In the team meeting yesterday..."
- Behavior: "When you interrupted Sarah three times..."
- Impact: "It shut down her thinking and made her less likely to contribute later"
- Opens conversation: "I wanted to check my observation with you"

**Elevator pitch** (60-second compelling story):
- Hook: Problem statement or intriguing question
- Solution: Your idea/proposal
- Benefit: What changes if adopted
- Ask: What you want listener to do

**Executive communication**: Adapt style for executives
- Lead with conclusion and key metrics
- Acknowledge constraints (time, information)
- Highlight risks and mitigants explicitly
- Request specific decisions, not just input
- Prepare for tough questions
