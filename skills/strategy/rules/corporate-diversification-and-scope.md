**Definition**: Corporate strategy determines what portfolio of businesses a company should operate and how to manage them for overall value creation.

#### Related vs. Unrelated Diversification

**Related Diversification**

- Enter businesses sharing capabilities, customers, or supply chains with core
- Leverages existing strengths

**Advantages**:

- Synergies reduce costs (shared distribution, procurement, operations)
- Knowledge transfer across businesses
- Economies of scope
- Shared capabilities apply to multiple businesses
- Brand reputation transfers

**Risks**:

- Success in one doesn't guarantee success in adjacent businesses
- Synergy overoptimism leads to overpaying for acquisitions
- Organizational complexity increases

**Examples**: 

- Coca-Cola: Beverages (cola → juice → water → sports drinks)
- Toyota: Automotive + financial services + logistics
- Samsung: Electronics + semiconductors + displays

**Unrelated Diversification (Conglomerate)**

- Own portfolio of unrelated businesses with no obvious synergies
- Financial engineering and portfolio approach

**Advantages**:

- Risk diversification (one business downturn offset by others)
- Financial engineering across portfolio
- Opportunistic M&A of undervalued businesses

**Disadvantages**:

- No synergies; costs rise
- Organizational complexity
- Conglomerate discount: market values below sum of parts
- Investor skepticism

**Examples**:

- Berkshire Hathaway: Insurance, railroads, utilities, manufacturing
- Tata Group: Steel, automobiles, IT services, hotels, power
- Reliance: Petrochemicals, oil & gas, retail, telecom

#### Diversification Discount

**Finding**: Diversified companies often trade below sum of their parts

**Why**:

- Investors can diversify better themselves through index funds
- Conglomerate management makes poor capital allocation decisions
- Opacity: Complex companies hard to value
- Bureaucracy: Corporate overhead erodes profitability

**Exception**: Berkshire Hathaway trades at PREMIUM because markets trust management to allocate capital

#### Synergy Identification and Valuation

**Types of Synergies**:

**Revenue synergies**:

- Cross-selling: Sell product A to customers of product B
- Distribution leverage: Use existing sales force for new products
- Brand extension: Leverage reputation into new categories
- Complementary products: Combined products create unique value

Reality: Revenue synergies hardest to achieve; customers don't automatically buy new products

**Cost synergies**:

- Procurement: Larger company negotiates better prices
- Manufacturing: Shared facilities, eliminate redundant capacity
- Overhead: Consolidate back-office, reduce duplicates
- Technology: Shared platforms, R&D collaboration

Reality: Easier to quantify but take longer to realize; integration costs substantial

**Financial synergies**:

- Tax benefits: Loss carryforwards, tax-efficient capital structure
- Lower cost of capital: Larger combined entity borrows more cheaply
- Capital allocation: Corporate realizes arbitrage opportunities

**Valuation discipline**:

1. List synergies specifically (e.g., "consolidate manufacturing in 2 of 4 facilities")
2. Quantify timing (when do they materialize?)
3. Assign probability (what's the risk?)
4. Calculate NPV of synergies
5. Don't pay more than: Standalone value + Synergy value

#### BCG Portfolio Matrix

Framework for managing business portfolio based on market growth and market share.


|                | **High Growth** | **Low Growth** |
| -------------- | --------------- | -------------- |
| **High Share** | STARS           | CASH COWS      |
| **Low Share**  | QUESTION MARKS  | DOGS           |


**Stars** (High growth, high share):

- Growing markets where company leads
- Invest to maintain position
- Generate good returns and future cash

**Cash Cows** (Low growth, high share):

- Mature markets where company dominates
- Harvest profits; limit reinvestment
- Lower growth but stable cash generation

**Question Marks** (High growth, low share):

- Growing markets but company is weak
- Invest to build share, or divest
- High risk, high potential return

**Dogs** (Low growth, low share):

- Mature markets where company is weak
- Generate little value
- Divest or harvest minimally

**Portfolio implications**: Invest in stars and promising question marks; harvest cash cows; divest dogs

#### GE Matrix (More Nuanced)

Uses business strength (internal) and industry attractiveness (external):

- **Invest**: High strength + attractive industry
- **Maintain**: Medium position or attractiveness
- **Divest**: Weak position + unattractive industry

More nuanced than BCG but requires more judgment.

#### Ansoff Matrix (Growth Strategies)

Strategic options for growth:


|                      | **Existing Products** | **New Products**    |
| -------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------- |
| **Existing Markets** | Market Penetration    | Product Development |
| **New Markets**      | Market Development    | Diversification     |


**Market Penetration**: Increase market share in existing markets with existing products

- Tactics: Competitive pricing, increased marketing, distribution expansion
- Lowest risk; uses existing capabilities

**Product Development**: Introduce new products to existing markets

- Tactics: R&D, feature addition, new variants
- Moderate risk; leverages existing distribution

**Market Development**: Enter new markets with existing products

- Tactics: Geographic expansion, new customer segments
- Moderate risk; leverages existing product capabilities

**Diversification**: Enter new markets with new products

- Tactics: Related diversification (leverage capabilities) or unrelated (portfolio approach)
- Highest risk; requires new capabilities and market knowledge
