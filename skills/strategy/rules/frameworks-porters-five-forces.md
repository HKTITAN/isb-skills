**Definition**: A framework analyzing the competitive intensity and profitability of an industry by examining five competitive forces.

#### Five Forces Model

**1. Threat of New Entrants**
- New competitors reduce incumbent profitability
- **Barriers to entry** determine threat level:
  - Capital requirements (pharmaceuticals, semiconductors need billions)
  - Economies of scale (leaders have cost advantage; difficult for small entrants)
  - Brand loyalty and switching costs (Coca-Cola dominance, Apple ecosystem)
  - Proprietary technology and patents (intellectual property moat)
  - Access to distribution (shelf space scarcity, established networks)
  - Regulatory barriers (licenses, permits, compliance requirements)
  - Absolute cost advantages (proprietary sourcing, learning curves)
- **Analysis**: High barriers = profitable incumbents; low barriers = contestable market
- **Example**: Aircraft manufacturing has high barriers (Boeing, Airbus duopoly); consulting has low barriers

**2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers**
- Suppliers control cost structure; high power = pressure on profitability
- **Suppliers gain power when**:
  - Few suppliers dominate (TSMC for semiconductors, Alphabet for mobile OS)
  - Switching to alternatives is expensive or difficult
  - Suppliers serve many industries (less dependent on any one buyer)
  - Supplier products are unique or differentiated
  - Suppliers can integrate forward (backward integration threat)
  - Buyer purchases represent small fraction of supplier revenue
- **Suppliers lose power when**:
  - Many alternatives exist (commodity suppliers)
  - Buyer can backward integrate (make inputs internally)
  - Buyer has scale to demand better terms
- **Example**: Apple has enormous power over component suppliers; Intel had enormous power over PC manufacturers until AMD competed

**3. Bargaining Power of Buyers**
- Customers can reduce profitability through price pressure
- **Buyers gain power when**:
  - Few large buyers dominate (Walmart negotiates aggressively)
  - Products are commoditized and interchangeable
  - Switching to alternatives is easy and cheap
  - Buyers can integrate backward (private label, in-house production)
  - Buyer purchases represent significant cost (price sensitivity increases)
  - Product is undifferentiated (little reason to pay premium)
- **Buyers lose power when**:
  - Highly differentiated products (loyalty reduces switching)
  - Strong brand or switching costs (Apple, enterprise software)
  - Many fragmented buyers (individual consumers)
  - Products represent small fraction of buyer cost
- **Example**: Walmart has enormous power over suppliers; individual consumers buying luxury goods have little power

**4. Threat of Substitutes**
- Substitutes satisfy same customer need differently; limit pricing power
- **Substitutes are threatening when**:
  - Substitutes offer similar or better value
  - Switching costs to substitutes are low
  - Substitute prices are competitive
  - Substitute quality is improving
- **Examples**: Email substitutes for fax; video conferencing substitutes for business travel; streaming substitutes for cinema
- **Analysis**: Monitor for weak signals of substitute emergence; consider indirect competitors

**5. Rivalry Among Existing Competitors**
- Intensity of competition shapes profitability and competitive dynamics
- **Rivalry is intense when**:
  - Many competitors of similar size (fragmented market)
  - Slow industry growth (competition for market share)
  - High fixed costs (pressure to maintain utilization)
  - High switching costs for customers (creates incentive to retain)
  - Undifferentiated products (price-based competition)
  - Competitors have different home countries, values, or objectives
  - Exit barriers are high (expensive to leave; forced to stay and fight)
  - Capacity added in large increments (supply volatility)
- **Rivalry is gentler when**:
  - Few large firms share market (mutual forbearance)
  - Rapid growth (room for multiple winners)
  - Differentiated products (brand loyalty reduces switching)
  - Exit is easy (low-cost to redeploy assets)
- **Example**: Airline industry has intense rivalry; luxury goods have gentler rivalry

#### Limitations and Extensions

- **Static analysis**: Captures moment in time; industries evolve
- **Interconnected forces**: Forces interact in complex ways not captured separately
- **Digital disruption**: Network effects and platform dynamics don't fit traditional framework
- **Modern extensions**: Add complementors (companies making your product more valuable)
