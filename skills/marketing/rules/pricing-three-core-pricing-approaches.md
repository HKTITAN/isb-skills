#### Cost-Based Pricing

Cost-plus pricing adds a markup percentage to production costs.

Formula: Price = Cost per Unit + (Markup % × Cost per Unit)

Example: If a t-shirt costs $5 to manufacture, a 100% markup yields $10 selling price.

Pros:
- Simple to implement and understand
- Ensures profit margin on every unit
- Fair pricing approach for transparent cost structures
- Predictable profitability

Cons:
- Ignores customer value perception (may overprice or underprice)
- In competitive markets, cost-plus can force prices below what customers will pay
- Ignores demand elasticity
- Doesn't capture differentiation value

Works best when:
- Value is genuinely related to cost (commodities)
- Costs are transparent (government contracts)
- Markets are competitive with limited price variation possible
- Costs scale predictably

#### Competition-Based Pricing

Sets price based on what competitors charge.

Approaches:
- **Price parity**: Match competitor prices
- **Price leadership**: Premium pricing as category benchmark
- **Underpricing**: Compete on price

Pros:
- Prevents leaving money on table through underpricing
- Prevents losing customers by pricing too high
- Relatively simple—easy to monitor

Cons:
- Risk of price wars destroying profitability
- Ignores your differentiation
- If all competitors use competitive pricing, race to bottom accelerates

Works best when:
- Products are genuinely similar (commodities, me-too offerings)
- Price is primary decision criterion
- You have cost parity with competitors
- Brand differentiation is minimal

#### Value-Based Pricing

Sets price based on value the offering creates for customers, not production costs.

Core principle: Price = Value Perceived by Customer

Example: A software reduces customer's data processing time by 50%. If that time savings costs customer $100K/year in labor, then software should price in the $20-40K/year range (capturing 20-40% of value created while leaving customer with significant benefit).

Willingness to Pay (WTP) Analysis:
- Maximum price customer will accept before choosing alternative
- Varies by segment, use case, customer characteristics
- Determined by: next best alternative cost, relative value vs. alternative, price sensitivity, ability to pay

Research methods for determining WTP:
- **Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter**: Four questions at varying prices to identify acceptable range
- **Conjoint Analysis**: Present product configurations with prices; measure preferences and value trade-offs
- **Gabor-Granger Method**: Sequential pricing ladder; determine optimal price maximizing revenue
- **Auction/Bidding Studies**: Real or simulated auctions reveal true WTP
- **Focus Groups/Interviews**: Qualitative discussion of price acceptability
- **Marketplace Testing**: Test different prices; observe actual demand

Pros:
- Captures value you create (higher profit than cost-plus)
- Aligns price with customer value perception
- Maximizes revenue when value varies across segments

Cons:
- Requires research to understand customer value
- Customers must perceive and agree with value
- Value must be sustainable and hard to copy
- Risk of overpricing if value perception is weak

Works best when:
- Innovation: New products where cost-plus doesn't apply
- Customized offerings: Tailored solutions with different value by customer
- B2B and enterprise: Customers evaluate ROI
- Premium/luxury: Value-driven buying, less price-sensitive
- Professional services: Value of outcome justifies fees
- SaaS and subscriptions: Value tied to usage and outcomes
