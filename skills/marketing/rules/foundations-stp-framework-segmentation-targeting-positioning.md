STP moves from broad market definition to focused strategy. It answers: Who are we serving, and how do we differentiate?

#### Segmentation: Dividing the Market

Segmentation is dividing a heterogeneous market into distinct groups with different needs, behaviors, or characteristics.

**Why segment?**

- Not all customers want the same thing at the same price or value proportion
- Tailored offerings are more effective than mass-marketing to everyone
- Segment leaders can capture disproportionate value by serving specific groups better
- Price differences: Different segments have different willingness-to-pay (WTP)

**Segmentation variables:**

- **Geographic**: Region, city/rural, climate, country, urban density
- **Demographic**: Age, gender, income, education, family size, occupation, social status
- **Psychographic**: Lifestyle, values, attitudes, personality types, aspirations, beliefs
- **Behavioral**: Purchase frequency, brand loyalty, usage rate, decision criteria, occasion for use, benefits sought
- **Needs-based**: Underlying customer needs and benefits sought (often most actionable for marketing)

Example: Pain reliever market segments by:

- Fast relief segment: Commuters needing quick relief during workday
- Long-lasting segment: Office workers wanting sustained effect
- Gentle segment: Health-conscious consumers worried about side effects
- Generic segment: Price-conscious customers willing to trade brand for savings

**Good segments share traits:**

- Distinct: Meaningfully different needs, behaviors, or value drivers
- Measurable: Can identify and size the segment with available data
- Accessible: Reachable through marketing and distribution channels
- Actionable: Large enough to justify tailored marketing investment
- Stable: Won't shift dramatically in short term

A segment of 10 customers in niche market may be too small to serve profitably. A segment that will consolidate or disappear isn't worth targeting.

#### Targeting: Choosing Attractive Segments

Targeting selects which segments represent attractive opportunities for the business.

**Targeting strategies:**

- **Undifferentiated (mass marketing)**: Offer one solution to all customers
  - Best for: Commodity products (salt, sugar), truly homogeneous markets
  - Pros: Lower costs, simpler operations, maximum scale
  - Cons: Weak differentiation, vulnerable to targeted competitors
- **Differentiated**: Target multiple segments with tailored offerings
  - Best for: Heterogeneous markets with different customer needs
  - Example: Car manufacturers offer economy, family, luxury, performance segments
  - Pros: Higher revenue, multiple competitive positions, stronger loyalty
  - Cons: Higher marketing/operations complexity, potential cannibalization
- **Focused (niche)**: Concentrate on one or two specific segments
  - Best for: Strong competitive advantage in specific segment
  - Example: Tesla initially focused on premium/luxury electric vehicle segment
  - Pros: Deep expertise, strong positioning, resource efficiency
  - Cons: Limited scale, vulnerable to larger competitors entering segment
- **Micro-targeting**: Data-driven targeting of highly specific clusters
  - Best for: Digital platforms with rich customer data
  - Example: Facebook/Google targeting by browsing history, location, interests, behaviors
  - Pros: Efficient spend, personalized messaging, high conversion potential
  - Cons: Privacy concerns, data dependency, complex implementation

**Segment evaluation criteria:**

- **Size**: Is segment large enough to support profitable operations?
- **Growth**: Is it growing or declining? What's the trajectory? (Declining segments tempting due to lower competition, but limited upside)
- **Competition**: How intense is competition? How many players? Is it saturated?
- **Fit**: Do we have distinctive competency to serve this segment better than competitors?
- **Profitability**: Can we achieve adequate returns? Will our cost structure work?
- **Strategic fit**: Does serving this segment align with our vision, brand, and capabilities?
- **Barriers to entry**: What protects leaders in this segment? Can we establish defensible advantage?

#### Positioning: Differentiation in Customer Minds

Positioning is how the brand is perceived relative to competitors in target customers' minds. It's not what you say about yourself; it's what customers believe based on total experience.

**Points of Parity (POPs) vs. Points of Difference (PODs):**

POPs are attributes customers expect from any credible brand. They're table stakes—you must deliver them but can't differentiate on them.

Examples:

- Smartphone POPs: Screen, processor, battery, camera, app ecosystem
- Hotel POPs: Clean rooms, check-in service, customer support
- Automobile POPs: Engine, wheels, safety features, seats

POPs vary by segment. Budget hotel POPs (clean functional room) differ from luxury hotel POPs (luxury amenities, personalized service, premium locations).

PODs are unique, valued attributes competitors don't deliver or can't easily replicate. They create competitive advantage.

Characteristics of strong PODs:

- **Relevant**: Target customers care about this attribute
- **Distinctive**: Competitors don't deliver it (or deliver weakly)
- **Defensible**: Hard to copy due to patents, proprietary tech, brand reputation, scale advantage, network effects
- **Believable**: Customers can perceive and verify the difference
- **Communicable**: You can clearly explain why the difference matters

Strong POD examples:

- Apple iPhone: Ecosystem integration (seamless hardware-software-services experience hard to replicate)
- Tesla: EV performance + Supercharger network (both technologically and operationally difficult to replicate)
- Southwest Airlines: Low fares + fun brand + point-to-point routing (operational model hard to copy at scale)
- Patagonia: Environmental sustainability commitment (brand reputation, supply chain control hard to fake)

Weak PODs are easily copied features, irrelevant attributes, or unbelievable claims. Avoid them.

**Positioning statement structure:**

"For [target segment], [brand] is the [category] that [unique value proposition]. Unlike [main competitor(s)], we [primary points of difference] because [reasons to believe/proof points]."

Example: "For environmentally-conscious, tech-savvy drivers, Tesla is the electric vehicle brand that combines performance with zero emissions. Unlike traditional luxury manufacturers, we innovate continuously in battery technology, autonomous driving, and charging infrastructure because we believe the future is electric and we're engineering it today."

**Building credible positioning requires alignment across the marketing mix:**

- **Product**: Must genuinely deliver on the positioning promise
- **Price**: Should align with positioning (premium positioning requires premium price; value positioning requires competitive pricing)
- **Distribution**: Where/how you sell shapes perception (luxury goods in discount channels damages positioning)
- **Promotion**: Messaging must consistently communicate PODs across all channels

Misalignment erodes positioning. Premium product + value pricing + commodity distribution + unclear messaging = confused customer perception.

**Repositioning** is sometimes necessary when:

- Market shifts make original positioning irrelevant
- New customer segments offer better opportunity
- Negative associations damage the brand
- Competitive pressure requires differentiation

Repositioning is risky and expensive. It requires real change (product, service, experience improvements), not just messaging change. It takes 2-3 years minimum and sustained investment.
